## Problem

Pharmaceutical marketing is written for doctors, but the people writing it
rarely get to talk to many. Message testing means recruiting real healthcare
professionals, which is slow, expensive and heavily governed — so most
campaign copy gets refined on instinct and a handful of anecdotes.

The Vaccines Commercial team I was embedded with wanted something they could
rehearse on: a chatbot that answered as a specific kind of doctor would, out of
the company's own research and literature rather than out of a general model's
memory. There was no internal precedent for it. Nobody handed me a spec.

## What I built

A retrieval-augmented chatbot that plays a healthcare-professional persona and
answers grounded in the client's own document library. The work was mine end to
end: framing the problem with the marketing team, deciding the architecture,
building it, measuring it, and shipping it over their local network for
validation.

It was validated by 10 users from the team, and then handed to a large
consultancy to take through a production build-out — which is the outcome this
kind of internal prototype is aiming for.

### Architecture

The interesting part isn't that it retrieves. It's that a single retrieval
strategy was never good enough, so the system decides how to search before it
searches.

## Hard parts and decisions

- **Building the evaluation framework before tuning anything.** This is the
  decision I'd defend hardest. RAG systems are easy to fiddle with and almost
  impossible to improve blind — every change makes some answers better and some
  worse, and without a fixed scorecard you optimise for whichever example you
  happened to test. I wrote the scenarios and the failure paths first, then
  iterated chunking, embedding and search strategy against measured results.
  The system reached **94% accuracy** on that evaluation set. The number matters
  much less than the fact that there was a number.
- **Hybrid retrieval, because embeddings lose the exact words.** Semantic search
  is excellent at "what do physicians worry about with this class of vaccine"
  and unreliable at a specific brand name or trial code, where near-miss
  neighbours look identical in vector space. BM25 handles exactly those. Running
  both and merging was worth the extra complexity; picking one would have meant
  choosing which half of the questions to answer badly.
- **Routing instead of one universal pipeline.** Once both retrieval modes
  existed, the question became which to use when — and that judgment is the kind
  of thing a model is genuinely good at and a heuristic is not. Routing agents
  send the query down the appropriate path, which kept each path simple enough
  to reason about instead of accreting special cases.
- **The PDFs were the real project.** Most of the early difficulty wasn't in
  retrieval at all; it was in structured extraction from research documents that
  were designed to be read by humans, not parsed. Retrieval quality is bounded
  by what survives that step, which is why it's the first stage in the diagram
  and not a footnote.
- **Working without a precedent.** No template existed internally, so the first
  weeks were spent with the marketing team turning "could a chatbot do message
  testing" into something testable. Getting to a defensible definition of what
  "good" meant was harder than any individual technical decision, and it's what
  made the evaluation framework possible.

## Also at Aspect Ratio

Fifteen months embedded with the same client's marketing, medical and global
bidding teams produced more than one thing:

- **A tender guidance tool for the global vaccines bidding team**, spanning
  **40+ countries and 5 vaccines**, putting pricing and bid-strategy guidance at
  the point where a bid decision is actually made rather than in a document
  nobody opens mid-deadline.
- **Four web scrapers** turning undocumented conference sites — no API, no
  export, structure changing between years — into structured datasets the
  medical team could query.
- **An end-to-end SQL analysis of vaccine-effectiveness data** that sat behind a
  European surveillance report.

## What this taught me

Being embedded changes what the job is. The hard part was rarely the model; it
was sitting with the people who'd use the thing, long enough to work out what
they actually needed, and then being honest about what the technology could and
couldn't deliver against that. The chatbot got handed on for production because
it had been validated by real users and measured against a real evaluation set —
not because it demoed well.
